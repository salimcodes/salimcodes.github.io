Reading the serve: what happens when AI tries to understand tennis?

Six models, twelve tennis strokes, and one question none of them could answer.

Before we beginWhat this is, and what it is not

This is not a story about building an AI model, getting an impressive accuracy score, and declaring that we have solved a problem. It is not a claim that computer vision can now replace tennis coaches or understand every movement on a tennis court.

Instead, it is a story about an experiment. We wanted to understand how well machines could recognise tennis actions, particularly the subtle differences between different types of serves. We tested several approaches, compared trained models with powerful off-the-shelf vision models, examined where they failed, and discovered that a problem we initially thought was relatively straightforward was considerably more difficult than expected.

There is something particularly interesting about this kind of experiment: sometimes the most valuable result is not the one you hoped to obtain, but the one that helps you understand why the problem is difficult in the first place.

And this is precisely what happened.

01 · The problemEvery point starts with a serve

Imagine a tennis match approaching its final moments. A player is serving at a crucial point, perhaps facing a break point against an opponent who has been returning aggressively throughout the match.

The server has options. A flat serve can be hit with considerable pace, giving the receiver very little time to react. A slice serve can move laterally, pulling the receiver away from the centre of the court. A kick serve can bounce high and force the receiver into an uncomfortable position.

These are not merely different ways of hitting a tennis ball. They are different tactical decisions.

Now imagine being the coach reviewing footage of that match. You want to know how frequently your opponent serves wide on the deuce side, whether they favour a slice serve under pressure, or whether their second serve is predominantly a kick serve.

That information could change how your player prepares for the next encounter. It could influence positioning, anticipation, return strategy, and even the tactical decisions made during a match.

But there is a problem. Someone has to watch the footage and identify every serve.

Across an entire tournament, this becomes a tedious annotation exercise. The more matches you analyse, the more difficult it becomes to maintain consistency and scale the process.

What if a computer vision system could automatically watch these videos and identify the type of serve being performed?

That is the question we wanted to investigate.

02 · ContextTennis has already shown us what computer vision can do

Before attempting to solve this problem, it is worth appreciating how much computer vision has already changed professional sports.

Consider a familiar situation in tennis. A player hits a powerful shot towards the baseline. The ball lands extremely close to the line. The line judge calls it out, the player disagrees, and suddenly the entire stadium is waiting for a decision.

For years, these moments depended heavily on human observation. Then came Hawk-Eye.

Using multiple cameras positioned around the court, Hawk-Eye tracks the ball and reconstructs its trajectory to estimate where it lands relative to the court boundaries. This technology transformed line calling, giving players, officials, and spectators a way to examine decisions that would otherwise be incredibly difficult to judge with the naked eye.

The Australian Open adopted electronic line calling across its courts in 2021. The ATP subsequently introduced live electronic line calling across its Tour-level events in 2025, while Wimbledon moved to electronic line calling in the same year.

The distinctionHawk-Eye answers where the ball went. We wanted to know what the player did.

The important distinction here is that Hawk-Eye addressed the question of whether a ball was in or out. It did not solve the problem we were interested in. And tennis is hardly the only sport where computer vision is making this transition.

In football, computer vision supports player tracking and semi-automated offside decisions. In cricket, Hawk-Eye reconstructs ball trajectories to support LBW reviews. In gymnastics, Fujitsu’s Judging Support System uses 3D motion analysis to help judges assess complex movements and difficulty scores. In athletics, Intel’s 3D Athlete Tracking system demonstrated how ordinary video could be used to extract detailed biomechanical information about athletes during competition.

The common thread is that these systems do more than record sporting events. They extract information from visual data that can support decisions, analysis, and performance evaluation.

So, if computer vision can track a ball travelling at extraordinary speed, reconstruct an athlete’s movement in three dimensions, and assist with decisions in professional sport, surely identifying the type of tennis serve should be possible?

Well, we wanted to find out.

03 · The questionCan a machine actually read a serve?

At first glance, the task sounds relatively straightforward. A tennis serve is an overhead action. A forehand is a groundstroke. A volley is played before the ball bounces. Surely a sufficiently capable vision model should be able to distinguish them.

But then we have the more interesting problem: distinguishing between flat, slice, and kick serves. Try it yourself.

All three involve an overhead movement, a ball toss, coordinated body rotation, and a racket travelling towards the contact point. The differences are often concentrated in subtle details of racket orientation, contact mechanics, and the spin imparted to the ball.

A human watching the full action can use several sources of information, including the player’s movement, the racket, and the ball’s subsequent flight and bounce. A machine, however, can only learn from the information made available to it.

This led us to a few expectations before running the experiments:

  • Broad categories should be easy. Serves, groundstrokes and volleys involve substantially different whole-body dynamics.
  • Serve type should be hard. Flat, slice and kick serves share similar body movements, with some of their most important differences occurring around the moment of racket–ball contact.
  • Trained models should win. A model trained specifically on tennis action data should outperform general-purpose vision models that had not been trained for this particular task.

We designed the experiments to test these expectations.

04 · The experimentPutting different models to the test

We used the THETIS (Three-dimensional TennIs Shots) dataset, a tennis action recognition dataset containing sequences from 55 players and 12 different action classes:

  • Backhand and two-handed backhand
  • Backhand slice and backhand volley
  • Forehand flat, open stance, and slice
  • Forehand volley
  • Flat, slice, and kick serves
  • Smash

The dataset contains 8,374 sequences across its different recording modalities, including RGB video, depth, silhouettes, and 2D and 3D skeletal representations.

One detail that mattered laterThe players perform their actions without actually hitting a tennis ball.

We approached the problem through two broad experimental settings.

The trained models

We trained X3D models using two different forms of input: RGB video and 3D skeletal data. The idea was to examine whether a model trained specifically on tennis actions could learn useful representations for recognising the different strokes. The video model was evaluated on 396 held-out clips, while the skeletal model was evaluated on 250 held-out clips.

We also trained a pose-based CNN and evaluated it on the same 60 clips we gave the frontier models (below), which makes it the most direct trained-versus-general comparison in the study.

The frontier vision models

We also wanted to understand how well powerful general-purpose vision models could perform without task-specific training. We selected 60 RGB clips, representing five examples from each of the 12 action classes. The filenames were replaced with randomly generated identifiers to prevent the models from exploiting the original naming conventions.

We evaluated three models, Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6 and Gemini 4 Hydrogen. Each model was given the videos and asked to identify the action being performed.

We then scored every model at three levels of granularity:

  • Fine-grained classification: identifying the exact action among all 12 classes.
  • Broad shot classification: identifying the broader category: groundstroke, volley, serve or overhead.
  • Serve type: on the serve clips only, whether the model named the right serve: flat, slice or kick.

This gave us an opportunity to examine not just whether a model could recognise a tennis action, but how performance changed as the distinctions became more subtle.

05 · The resultsThe models could see the shot, but could they read the serve?

This was where things became interesting.

For the 12-class task, the pose CNN reached 68% accuracy, the X3D video model 62% and the X3D skeletal model 53%. Among the general-purpose vision models, Claude achieved 52%, GPT-6 37% and Gemini 27%. With twelve classes, random guessing would score about 8%, so every model was learning something useful about tennis movements, although there was still considerable room for improvement.

But the more interesting finding emerged when we examined broad shot categories. The frontier models performed considerably better when asked only for the broader action, with Claude achieving 80%, GPT-6 87% and Gemini 72% (the trained models scored 76–90%). In other words, distinguishing between a serve, a volley, and a groundstroke was substantially easier than identifying the precise type of stroke.

Then came the serves. We took only the serve clips and checked whether each model named the right serve type. A model that recognises a serve but has no idea which kind will land near 33%, the score you get by guessing among three options.

The result was particularly interesting because the trained models had been built specifically for the task. They did beat the frontier models on the 12 strokes, just as we predicted, but that advantage vanished on serve type. Even with task-specific training, the models could not reliably tell the three serves apart.

This was not what we had hoped to see, but it raised a much more important question: why?

06 · The failuresWhat exactly were the models missing?

This is where the experiment became especially useful. Rather than stopping at the accuracy scores, we inspected individual predictions to understand what the models were getting wrong.

One example involved a slice serve. Claude classified it as a flat serve, GPT-6 also classified it as a flat serve, while Gemini predicted a forehand. Across the five slice-serve examples, Claude correctly identified none, GPT-6 identified one, and Gemini identified one.

Another example involved a smash. All three frontier models classified this smash as a serve, and the trained X3D models showed the same confusion in their predictions.

At first, this might seem like a simple classification error. But think about what the model actually sees. A serve and a smash can both involve a player extending their racket arm above their head and swinging downwards towards a ball. Without the ball and the surrounding interaction, the body movements can be remarkably similar. The model is being asked to distinguish between two actions while some of the most useful contextual information is missing.

We also observed confusion between forehands and backhands. GPT-6 confused the two 22 times across 40 groundstroke and volley examples, while Claude made this mistake once and Gemini made it 19 times.

These examples point towards a broader issue: recognising the overall movement is not necessarily the same as understanding the finer details that give an action its meaning.

07 · The limitationThe one we could not ignore: THETIS has no ball

If there is one limitation that fundamentally changes how we interpret these results, it is this. The THETIS dataset records players performing tennis movements, but they are not actually hitting a tennis ball. There is no ball trajectory, no spin, no bounce, and no racket–ball interaction.

Think about what separates a flat serve from a slice serve or a kick serve. The differences are not simply about where the player’s arm moves. They are also about how the racket contacts the ball and the spin that results from that contact. A player can perform a visually similar overhead movement while producing very different ball trajectories.

When the ball is absent, the model loses access to information that could be essential for making the distinction. This means we need to be careful about interpreting chance-level accuracy. It may reflect limitations in the available visual information, rather than simply indicating that the models are incapable of learning the task.

08 · What we learnedAnd where we go from here

The most important takeaway is that computer vision performance depends not only on the architecture or the model’s capacity, but also on whether the input representation contains the information required to solve the task.

Our models could recognise broad tennis actions considerably better than chance. However, when the task required distinguishing between movements with subtle differences, their performance deteriorated. The absence of the ball in THETIS provides a plausible explanation for part of this difficulty.

This leads naturally to our next experiment. We want to explore whether synthetic tennis movements generated in a controlled 3D environment, such as Unity, can help us investigate the importance of ball interaction.

By generating serve sequences with corresponding body movements and ball trajectories, we could compare what a model learns from body movement alone against what it learns when the ball and its behaviour are also available. We could potentially control the serve type, spin, racket orientation, and camera viewpoint, creating a setting where we know precisely what happened and can examine which visual signals are most useful for classification.

Status: this experiment has not yet been completed, but it is a natural next step arising from what we observed.

Final thoughtsThe mistakes are data too

I think there is a tendency in machine learning to focus heavily on the final accuracy score. We train a model, compare it against a baseline, report the result, and move on. But experiments like this remind us that the mistakes can be just as informative as the successes.

We started with a relatively simple question: can a machine distinguish between different types of tennis serves? What we found was that recognising a broad sporting action and understanding its finer mechanics are two very different problems. We also discovered how much the availability of contextual information can influence what a model is capable of learning.

Sometimes the problem is not that the machine cannot see well enough. It is that we have not given it enough of what it needs to understand.

And that is where the next experiment begins.
